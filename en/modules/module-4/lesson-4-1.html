4.1 — What Are Phenomena and Why Do We Need Them

Module 4 • 15 min

🌀 What Are Hypnotic Phenomena

Hypnotic phenomena are unusual states and reactions that occur in trance: catalepsy, amnesia, hallucinations, age regression, anesthesia, and others. They are tools that the hypnotist uses to demonstrate the power of trance and perform therapeutic work.

Phenomena are not "tricks" or "paranormal events." They are natural physiological and psychological reactions of the brain in an altered state of consciousness. Anyone capable of entering a sufficiently deep trance can exhibit these phenomena — provided certain conditions are met.

In this lesson, we will cover the five conditions necessary for any phenomenon and the two types of tests used to check a subject's readiness.

✅ Five Conditions for Any Phenomenon

According to Jerry Kein's (Kayfabe) methodology, five conditions must be met for any hypnotic phenomenon to manifest successfully.

1️⃣ Absence of Fears and Misconceptions

First and most important. If the subject is afraid of hypnosis, believes it to be "mind control," or buys into myths ("I won't be able to come out of trance," "the hypnotist will have power over me") — no phenomenon will work. Fear blocks the ability to go under.

What to do: before any work, give a brief explanation. Dispel the myths. Explain that hypnosis is a natural state in which a person retains control and free will. Answer all questions before starting.

Example: "Hypnosis is simply a state of deep relaxation and focus. You can open your eyes, get up, and leave at any moment. You won't lose control — on the contrary, you'll be managing your own state, and I'll just be a guide."

2️⃣ Desire to Enter Hypnosis

The subject must want to enter trance. If they agreed out of politeness or under pressure, the depth of trance will be insufficient. Motivation like "let's see what this is about" works worse than "I want to experience this state."

Example check: "On a scale from 0 to 10, how much do you want to go into hypnosis right now?" A score of 7+ is a good indicator. 5 or below — work on motivation.

3️⃣ Trust in the Operator

The subject must trust the hypnotist. Without trust, there is no depth. The subject's subconscious "scans" the operator: is it safe? Are they competent? Will they cause harm?

What builds trust: a confident voice, calm demeanor, no rushing, honest answers to questions, respect for boundaries, demonstrated experience (or at least knowledge).

4️⃣ Somnambulism (Confirmed by a Covert Test)

The subject must reach a somnambulistic level of trance. Not "light trance" or "medium" — this is a deep level where the body's automatisms begin to work without conscious control. It is confirmed not by the subject's words ("I think I'm deep"), but by covert tests.

5️⃣ Openness to "Play" (Readiness for Experiment)

The subject must want to "play" — be open to the experiment, ready to accept an unusual experience. If the person is skeptical ("come on, prove it") or passively waiting — the phenomenon may not work. Lightness, curiosity, and a willingness to be surprised are needed.

Example: "We're going to try an interesting experiment now. Even if nothing happens — that's still an experience. Just allow yourself to play and observe what happens."

🔍 Covert vs Overt Tests

To check whether the subject is ready for a phenomenon demonstration, trance depth tests are used. They fall into two types.

🙈 Covert Tests

The subject does not know they are being tested. They don't evaluate their own result, don't worry about failure. This gives a clean reading of trance depth without distortion.

Examples of covert tests:

  • Number amnesia (in Elman induction): during Elman induction, the hypnotist names a number and gives the suggestion: "You won't remember this number." After emerging, they ask: "What number did I ask you to remember?" If the subject doesn't recall — test passed.
  • Fractionation: the hypnotist repeatedly takes the subject in and out of trance, deepening it each time. The subject thinks it's just a deepening technique — unaware that their reaction to returning to trance is diagnostic.
  • Ideomotor signals: "If your subconscious is ready, one finger will lift by itself." The subject may not realize they're moving a finger — this is a covert test for automatisms.

👁️ Overt Tests

The subject knows they are being tested and sees the result. They can evaluate whether it worked or not.

Examples of overt tests:

  • Eyelid catalepsy: "Your eyelids are so tightly closed that you cannot open them. Try — can you?" The subject tries and sees the result.
  • Name amnesia: "When I ask you what your name is, you won't be able to remember. Try — what is your name?" The subject consciously tries to recall.
  • Hand levitation: "Your hand is becoming light and rising into the air by itself." The subject watches their hand.

Overt tests are a demonstration. The subject sees that hypnosis works, and their trust grows. But there is a downside.

⚕️ Why Only Covert Tests in Therapy

In therapeutic practice, overt tests are strongly discouraged. Here's why:

  1. Risk of failure: the client may not accept the suggestion (eyelids didn't lock, hand didn't lift). In an overt test, this is perceived as a personal defeat. "I couldn't do it — so I'm a bad client, something's wrong with me."
  2. Client context: people come to therapy because they've already tried "everything" — they're tired of failure. Adding another one is cruel and counterproductive.
  3. Our goal is positivity: a therapeutic session should leave a feeling of success and resource. An overt test, even a successful one, shifts the client's attention from their internal process to an external "check."

Instead: use covert tests during induction (number amnesia, fractionation, ideomotor signals). You see the result — the client doesn't. If the test is passed, you know the depth is sufficient for the phenomenon. If not, you simply deepen the trance, and the client never suspects that anything "didn't work."

Example of therapeutic approach: the hypnotist performs Elman induction with a number. After emerging, they ask: "Do you remember the number?" — "No." — "Great, your trance was deep enough. Now we can work." That's it. No evaluation, no "passed/failed."

🎉 In Demonstration — You Can and Should

Overt tests work great for demonstration purposes:

  • With friends and acquaintances: show how hypnosis works, impress, break stereotypes.
  • At parties: hand catalepsy, levitation, name amnesia — spectacular and fun.
  • For training: in a study group, overt tests provide tangible proof that the technique works.
  • When the person is ready to play: if the subject is up for an experiment and not afraid of "failure," an overt test becomes part of the game.

The main rule: overt tests are for demonstration, covert tests are for therapy. Don't confuse the contexts.

📈 The Law of Compounding for Phenomena

One of the key principles of working with phenomena is the law of compounding (accumulation). It states: each subsequent phenomenon strengthens the previous one.

How it works: when a subject successfully exhibits one phenomenon, their confidence grows, trance depth increases, and the next phenomenon manifests more easily and vividly. Each success "builds on" the previous one, creating an amplifying effect.

Example sequence (in demonstration):

  1. Eyelid catalepsy → subject can't open eyes. Success.
  2. Hand catalepsy → hand freezes in the air. Deeper.
  3. Name amnesia → can't remember their name. Even deeper.
  4. Positive hallucination → sees a non-existent object. Maximum depth.

Each step amplifies the effect of the previous one. If we'd started directly with step 4, the chances of success would be lower. Compounding is the principle of "warming up."

In therapy, compounding also works but remains invisible to the client: covert test → therapeutic suggestion → post-hypnotic anchor. Each stage prepares the next.

📝 Practice Assignment

Task 1. Identify the five conditions for yourself

Imagine you need to demonstrate hand catalepsy to a friend. Go through the five conditions: do they have any fears? Is there desire? Do they trust you? How will you check trance depth with a covert test? Are they ready to "play"?

Task 2. Analyze covert vs overt test

Take any test from the lesson (e.g., number amnesia or eyelid catalepsy). Describe how it would look in a therapeutic context (covert) and in a demonstration context (overt). How does the wording of the suggestion differ? How does the subject's reaction differ?

Task 3. Create a compounding sequence

Design your own sequence of 3 phenomena for a demonstration with a friend. Write down which phenomena you chose and why in that particular order. How does each subsequent phenomenon build on the previous one?

Task 4. Reflection

Answer for yourself: which context is closer to you — therapy or demonstration? In which context do you plan to use phenomena in practice? Your answer will determine which tests and techniques to focus on in your learning.

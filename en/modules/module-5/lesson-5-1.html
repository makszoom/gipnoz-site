5.1 — Three Questions for the Client

Module 5 • 20 min

🎬 Video will be inserted after uploading to YouTube

🎯 Three Questions — The Foundation of Diagnosis

Before putting a client into hypnosis, you need to understand what you're working with. There are three key questions that define the entire suggestion strategy. Without them, you'll be "shooting blanks" — making a beautiful suggestion that doesn't hit the person's real request.

Here are the three questions:

  1. WHAT do you want? — A positive formulation of the desired state. Not through "don't."
  2. HOW will you know that you have it? — A specific, measurable criterion for the result.
  3. WHY do you want this? — Deep motivation, what it's for.

These three questions are the minimum set for diagnosis. They turn a vague complaint into a specific task for suggestion.

❓ Question 1 — WHAT do you want?

Most people formulate their request through negation: "I don't want to worry," "I want to stop being afraid," "I don't want to depend on others' opinions." The problem is that the subconscious doesn't hear the word "don't." When you say "don't be afraid," the subconscious hears "be afraid." When the client says "I don't want to worry," their subconscious fixates on worry.

The hypnotherapist's job is to reframe the request into a positive formulation:

  • "I don't want to worry" → "I want to feel calm and confident"
  • "I don't want to be afraid" → "I want to feel safe and at ease"
  • "I don't want to be dependent" → "I want to rely on myself and my decisions"

If the client struggles, help them: "Okay, what do you want instead of this?" or "If a magic wand solved your problem — how would you feel?"

Important: write down the client's exact words. Don't paraphrase in your own words. Their own words are the key to their subconscious.

📏 Question 2 — HOW will you know that you have it?

This is a question about measurable results. The client says "I want confidence" — that's an abstraction. How will they know confidence has appeared? What exactly will change in their behavior, well-being, reactions?

Examples of criteria:

  • "I'll stop checking my phone every 5 minutes waiting for a reply"
  • "I'll look the other person in the eye, not at the floor"
  • "My stomach will stop clenching when the boss calls me in"
  • "I'll be able to say 'no' without feeling guilty"

The more specific the criterion, the easier it is to build the suggestion. You'll use these images in trance: "you notice how your gaze becomes calm and steady... how your breathing stays even... how words come easily..."

Another technique — a 0–10 scale: "Where are you now? And where do you want to be?" — gives a number to aim for.

🔥 Question 3 — WHY do you want this?

This is a question about motivation. Why does the person want this change? What will it bring to their life? Without deep motivation, the client won't change — even in hypnosis.

It is emphasized: if the motivation is superficial ("my wife told me to," "it's trendy"), the result will be weak. A personal, emotionally charged reason is needed.

Examples of deep motivation:

  • "I want to be a role model for my children"
  • "I want to enjoy my work, not suffer through it"
  • "I want to feel free inside myself"
  • "I'm tired of being afraid — I just want to live"

A suggestion built on the answer to "why" hits the client's values directly. This provides the energy for change.

🗣️ Dialogue Example: Trouble Saying "No"

Client: "I can't refuse people. Everyone takes advantage of me, but I can't say 'no.'"

Therapist: "I understand. Let's go through the three questions. WHAT do you want instead of this? How would you like to feel and act when someone asks you for something?"

Client: "I want to feel calm when I refuse. So there's no guilt."

Therapist: "Great. And HOW will you know that you have it? What will change?"

Client: "Well... first I'll say 'let me think about it' instead of 'yes' right away. Then I'll be able to say 'no, that's not convenient.' And without a lump in my throat or the urge to apologize."

Therapist: "Wonderful criteria. And WHY do you want this? What will change in your life when you can calmly refuse?"

Client: "I'll have time for myself. I'll stop doing things I hate. I'll respect myself."

Therapist (to self — jotting down key words): "calm," "no guilt," "let me think about it," "say directly," "no lump in my throat," "time for myself," "respect myself." The suggestion in trance will be built from these words. No need for anyone else's words.

🧠 The Client's Words Matter More Than Scripts

A crucial principle: use the client's own words for suggestion. Don't replace them with your own terms. If the client said "lump in my throat" — use "lump in my throat." If they said "confidence and grounding" — use "confidence and grounding."

Why does this work? Because the client's words are already connected to their neural networks. When you speak them in trance, the client's subconscious recognizes its own language and accepts the suggestion without resistance. Someone else's words ("self-actualization," "resource state," "integration") create an analytical barrier.

Rule: don't invent eloquent formulations — use what the client said.

🔄 Problem vs Task

Most clients come with a problem — what they don't want. Your job is to translate this into a task — what they do want.

Problem (don't want) Task (want)
Don't want to fear public speaking Want to feel confident and engaged with the audience
Don't want to snap at my kids Want to respond calmly and with understanding
Don't want to depend on others' opinions Want to rely on my own inner judgment

A positive formulation is not wordplay. It's a direction for the subconscious. The subconscious moves toward images. If you give it an image of "calm and confidence" — it goes there. If you give it "don't be afraid" — it fixates on fear.

✍️ Practice: Practice the Three Questions with a Partner

Exercise. Pair work. One is the "client," the other is the "therapist."

  1. The client formulates any problem (real or fictional).
  2. The therapist asks the three questions in order, writing down the client's key words.
  3. The therapist reads back the answers and checks: are all three formulated positively?
  4. Switch roles.

Debrief after practice:

  • Were you able to translate the problem into a task?
  • Was the criterion (HOW will you know) specific? Or abstract?
  • Was there a deep motivation (WHY)? Or was the answer superficial?

Success criterion: After the three questions, you have a ready-made set of 5–10 client words and phrases to use in direct suggestion. If you didn't write down a single vivid word — the questions were asked formally, dig deeper.

6.1 — Stage Hypnosis

Module 6 • 20 min

🎬 Video will be inserted after uploading to YouTube

🎭 What is Stage Hypnosis

Stage hypnosis is a stage show in which the hypnotist uses the same induction techniques as in therapy, but with a fundamentally different goal: to entertain the audience, not to help a person. On stage, hypnosis is a tool of show business; in the office, it's a tool of help. And they must not be confused.

Many hypnotherapists started on stage, but not all stage hypnotists become good therapists — and vice versa. Nevertheless, understanding how stage hypnosis works gives the therapist valuable skills: the ability to quickly establish rapport, work with a group, instantly induce trance, and impressively demonstrate hypnotic phenomena.

👥 Selecting Volunteers — Who Comes on Stage

Stage hypnosis begins not with induction, but with selection. The hypnotist invites volunteers from the audience. But not everyone comes up — and that's no accident. Those who step on stage are people who:

  • Are willing to play. This is the key factor. The person internally agrees to be part of the show, is open to the experience, and isn't afraid to look silly.
  • Are not skeptics. Skeptics either don't come up at all, or — if they happen to be chosen — block the suggestion. The skeptic's subconscious doesn't give consent to trance.
  • Are highly suggestible. Among volunteers, approximately 15–20% are somnambulists (deep trance), the rest are light or medium trance.

"People who come on stage are people who want to come up. They're already ready to play. If someone isn't ready — they won't come up, and that's normal." Stage fright is a natural filter that weeds out those whose subconscious hasn't given consent.

Professional stage hypnotists additionally conduct suggestibility tests right on stage: hands sticking together, falling forward-backward, fingers locking. Those who respond best become the "stars of the show" — the others are gently sent back to the audience.

🎲 Willingness to Play — Not Everyone is Ready, and That's Okay

A crucial principle: hypnosis is cooperation. On stage, as in the office, the hypnotist cannot hypnotize someone who doesn't want to be. The difference is that on stage, refusal is obvious — the person simply doesn't come up.

Willingness to play includes:

  • Trust — the person believes the hypnotist won't do anything bad to them.
  • Openness to experience — readiness to undergo an unusual state.
  • Absence of fear of losing control — the most common reason for refusal.

If a person isn't ready — it doesn't mean they're "bad" or "too rational." It means their subconscious is not agreeing to this interaction at this moment. And that is absolutely normal. In the office, by the way, many who wouldn't come on stage go into deep trance — because in the office it's safe, private, and there's no audience.

🌀 Real Trance vs Acting: People on Stage Are Really in Trance

You often hear the opinion: "Stage hypnosis isn't real trance, people are just pretending." People on stage do enter a real hypnotic trance.

Evidence that it's not acting:

  • Catalepsy. A person is placed between two chairs — their body becomes rigid as a board. This can't be simulated without special training.
  • Analgesia. Volunteers don't feel pain from pricks or pinches — physiological reactions (pulse, blood pressure) confirm this.
  • Amnesia. After the show, some volunteers don't remember what they did on stage.
  • Post-hypnotic suggestions. A person carries out commands after coming out of trance — and this is not conscious behavior.

Key difference from therapy: on stage, trance is a tool of entertainment. The hypnotist uses deep hypnotic phenomena for impressive demonstrations. But the trance itself is the same as in the office. The difference is only in intention and context.

🔄 Post-hypnotic Reinduction on Stage — An Impressive Technique

One of the most spectacular techniques in stage hypnosis is post-hypnotic reinduction. The essence: the hypnotist gives a volunteer in trance a post-hypnotic suggestion — upon a specific signal (finger snap, word, clap) the person instantly returns to deep trance.

After bringing them out of trance, the hypnotist demonstrates to the audience that the volunteer is "normal" — talking, answering questions. Then gives the conditioned signal — and the person instantly "switches off," falling into trance.

This technique especially impresses audiences because:

  • It shows the automatic nature of the reaction — the person doesn't control it.
  • It demonstrates the power of rapport between the hypnotist and volunteer.
  • It creates an element of surprise — the audience sees that "this really works."

Therapeutic equivalent: post-hypnotic suggestions for reinforcing changes. But while on stage it's entertainment, in the office it's a working tool for long-term effect.

😱 "Scary Hypnosis": People's Fear of the Stage

Many are afraid of stage hypnosis. And this fear has specific reasons:

  • "They'll make me quack like a duck." The person fears the hypnotist will make them do humiliating things in front of an audience.
  • "I'll forget my own name." Fear of losing control over oneself, one's identity.
  • "I won't be able to come out of trance." The myth that you can get "stuck" in hypnosis forever.
  • "They'll find out my secrets." Fear of losing control over information about oneself.

All these fears are based on misunderstandings of the nature of hypnosis. A person in trance does not lose the ability to say "no". If a suggestion contradicts their values, they will come out of trance or simply not follow the command.

Furthermore, everything on stage happens with consent. The volunteer knows where they're going. The hypnotist cannot force a person to do something they don't want to — even in deep trance. If a suggestion causes strong inner protest, the subconscious simply blocks it.

The fear of stage hypnosis is the fear of the unknown. When a person understands that hypnosis is not a loss of control but a change of state, the fear goes away.

🏥 Difference from Therapy: Entertainment vs Help

It's important to understand the fundamental difference between stage and therapeutic hypnosis. These are different professions with different goals and methods.

Parameter Stage Hypnosis Therapeutic Hypnosis
Goal Entertain the audience Help the client
Selection Most suggestible, somnambulists Works with any level of suggestibility
Trance depth Maximum (for phenomena) Sufficient for change
Suggestions Showy, unusual Therapeutic, ecological
Ethics Don't humiliate, remove suggestions Informed consent, do no harm

A good hypnotherapist doesn't have to be able to work on stage. But understanding the mechanics of stage hypnosis gives them additional tools: rapid inductions, working with catalepsy, understanding what deep trance looks like.

⚖️ Ethics on Stage: Rules for a Professional Hypnotist

Even in an entertainment context, there are strict ethical boundaries that every stage hypnotist must follow:

  1. Don't humiliate. You can't make a volunteer do something that will cause them shame after coming out of trance. Quacking like a duck — okay, barking like a dog — okay, mimicking intimate movements — not okay. The boundary runs along the line of human dignity.
  2. No sexual suggestions. Categorical prohibition. Any suggestions of a sexual nature violate ethical norms and can be considered harassment. After the show, the volunteer may remember the suggestion and experience deep psychological discomfort.
  3. Remove all suggestions after the show. Every post-hypnotic suggestion must be canceled. The volunteer should not leave the stage with a "stuck" command. The exception is if the suggestion is harmless (e.g., feeling of lightness and energy), but it's better to remove it too if not agreed upon beforehand.
  4. Don't work with people under the influence of alcohol/drugs. This is not only ethically questionable but also dangerous for the volunteer's health.
  5. Return to normal. After the show, every volunteer must be fully brought out of trance and checked for residual suggestions. The hypnotist is responsible for the person's state after the show.

"The stage is entertainment. But entertainment built on trust. If you break that trust — you're not just a bad hypnotist, you're harming a real person. And that negates the entire show."

Stage hypnosis is an art. Like any performer, a stage hypnotist must remember that their "instrument" is living people with their feelings, fears, and dignity.

✍️ Practice: Analysis of a Stage Performance

Exercise. Find a video of stage hypnosis on YouTube (e.g., a professional hypnotist's performance). Watch 10–15 minutes of the show, analyzing:

  1. How does the hypnotist select volunteers? By what signs does he identify suggestible people?
  2. What induction techniques does he use? Rapid induction? Elman? Another method?
  3. What phenomena does he demonstrate? Catalepsy, analgesia, amnesia?
  4. Does he use post-hypnotic suggestions? Is there reinduction?
  5. Does he remove suggestions after the show? How exactly?

Success criterion: After watching, you can clearly identify at least 5 technical techniques used by the hypnotist and assess their compliance with ethical standards.

6.3 — Difficult Clients and Situations

Module 6 • 20 min

🎬 Video will be inserted after uploading to YouTube

🛡️ Who Are "Difficult Clients"

In a hypnotherapist's practice, sooner or later clients appear for whom standard techniques don't work. This topic is covered in a separate block because the ability to recognize such a client and respond correctly is a professional skill. A mistake can cost time, money, and — more importantly — reputation.

"Difficult" doesn't mean "bad" or "harmful." It means that for one reason or another, the standard hypnotic work protocol with this client is ineffective or impossible. The therapist's job is to notice this in time and make the right decision: adapt the approach, refer to another specialist, or honestly refuse to work.

👨‍👩‍👧‍👦 Close Relatives — The Worst Clients

This is stated directly and without compromise: don't work with close relatives. Hypnosis with them almost always fails, and there's a clear reason why.

A relative's subconscious doesn't take you seriously as a "hypnotist" or "therapist." To them, you're "Johnny who left his socks on the floor yesterday" or "my little girl I raised." The "therapist-client" role model doesn't form because there's a much older and stronger role model: "mother-child," "brother-brother," "husband-wife."

Why this happens:

  • No role separation. Hypnosis requires the client's subconscious to recognize you as an authoritative guide. With relatives, this is impossible — their subconscious remembers you "in diapers."
  • Too much shared history. Grievances, unspoken claims, family roles — all of this interferes with a clean therapeutic contact.
  • The client isn't internally ready. If a wife asks her husband-hypnotherapist to "hypnotize her to quit smoking," she doesn't actually want to quit — she wants her husband to stop nagging her. The subconscious senses this substitution.

Recommendation: if a relative approaches you — refer them to a colleague. Don't take money, don't try to "help as family." At best, you'll waste time; at worst, you'll ruin the relationship.

👴 Clients Over 60: The Authority Problem

A vivid example from practice: "I'm 30, they're 60 — I'm young enough to be their son." This isn't bragging, but a real problem. Older clients often struggle to accept the authority of a young specialist.

Main difficulties:

  • Generation gap. A 60+ client perceives hypnosis as "something dubious" and a 30-year-old therapist as a "green kid who learned from the internet."
  • Habit of directive medicine. Older people are used to the doctor being an unquestionable authority. If the therapist is young, this authority doesn't kick in.
  • Skepticism toward "new methods." "Hypnosis? Isn't that quackery?" — a typical reaction.

How to work with such clients:

  • Show competence from the first minute. No uncertainty. Confident, calm, professional tone.
  • Don't argue. If the client doubts — don't prove, but offer: "Let's just try it, and you can judge the result yourself."
  • Demonstrate phenomena. Hand catalepsy, levitation — visual proof that "something is happening."
  • Use age as an advantage. "I specialize in modern fast techniques. My approach is different from what you've seen in movies."

If the client categorically refuses to recognize your authority — it's better to refer them to an older colleague. This is not defeat, but a professional decision.

🕊️ People with Psychotrauma Blocking the Work

A case from practice: a woman with trauma who physically couldn't work with a male therapist. Every time he started induction, she came out of trance — her subconscious blocked any attempt to "submit" to a man.

This is a classic example of psychotrauma that blocks hypnotic work. When the trauma is related to the therapist's gender, age, appearance, or voice, the client's subconscious may reject any suggestions — not because the client is "resisting," but because a defense mechanism kicks in.

Signs of such blocking:

  • The client can't relax, even if they try.
  • Physical reactions appear during attempted induction: rapid breathing, muscle tension, tears.
  • The client reports that "something is in the way," "there's a barrier inside."
  • Repeated induction attempts don't improve the situation, but worsen it.

What to do in this situation:

  1. Don't push. Don't try to "break through" the blockage through willpower or more forceful techniques. This can worsen the trauma.
  2. Discuss it. Calmly ask: "I notice you're having trouble relaxing. What's happening?" The client may not connect their reaction to the trauma themselves, but conversation will clarify the situation.
  3. Refer. If the blockage is related to your gender, age, or other characteristics — honestly acknowledge it and refer to another specialist.

Refusing to work with such a client is not weakness, but professionalism. Referring a woman with trauma to a female therapist is the right decision that will help the client, not harm them.

🧠 "I Can't Be Hypnotized"

One of the most common problems — a client who believes they are unhypnotizable. They are convinced they have "too strong a will," "too analytical a mind," or "too skeptical a nature."

"People who say 'I can't be hypnotized' have usually tried before — and it didn't work. But the problem isn't them, it's how it was done to them."

Myths that hinder such clients:

  • "Hypnosis is loss of consciousness." In reality, hypnosis is a state of focused attention, not sleep.
  • "I can't be hypnotized because I'm always in control." The ability to control yourself doesn't hinder hypnosis. On the contrary, people with strong willpower often go into trance excellently — once they stop resisting.
  • "If I didn't pass out, then hypnosis didn't work." Deep trance without amnesia is normal. Many therapeutic changes happen precisely in light and medium trance.

Strategy for working with such clients:

  1. Don't argue. Don't try to prove the client wrong. Accept their position: "Alright, let's just check what happens when you relax."
  2. Rename it. Don't call it "hypnosis." Say: "Let's just do a relaxation exercise and see what happens."
  3. Use indirect techniques. Instead of direct induction — non-directive methods, Ericksonian turns, stories.
  4. Demonstrate small victories. "Your hand became lighter. Did you notice that?" — every small sign of trance becomes proof for the skeptic.

Important: if the client comes with a "prove it to me" attitude, you've already lost. Real work only begins when the client shifts from "prove it" to "let's try."

🚫 When Help Is Impossible

There are situations where a person wants to change but cannot follow the therapist's instructions. This isn't their fault — that's how the psyche works. Three main reasons why help may be impossible:

1️⃣ Psychotrauma

Deep trauma creates a "protective barrier" in the psyche that blocks any attempts at suggestion. The client's subconscious perceives hypnosis as a threat — even if the client sincerely wants to work. In such cases, not hypnotherapy but long-term work with a psychologist or psychotherapist specializing in trauma is needed.

2️⃣ Fear

There are people who are panically afraid of hypnosis. They may come to a session because they "have to" or "the doctor said so," but their subconscious is in a state of combat readiness. Any attempt at induction causes rapid heartbeat, sweating, a desire to run away. Working in such a state is impossible — fear blocks trance.

3️⃣ Deep Beliefs

Religious beliefs, cultural taboos, worldview positions — all of these can make hypnotherapy impossible. If a person sincerely believes that hypnosis is "demonic" or "loss of soul," no technique will help. Their subconscious will block suggestions at the level of core values.

"We are not omnipotent. Hypnosis is a powerful tool, but it doesn't work when a person isn't ready. And that's normal. Not every case is our case."

🗣️ How to Handle It: The Talk and Refunding Money

When you realize you can't help a client, you have a professional obligation to tell them honestly and directly. This is called "the talk" — a conversation every therapist should know how to conduct.

What to Tell the Client

  1. Honestly acknowledge your limitations. "I see that my methods aren't giving the result we want. I can't help you with this problem."
  2. Explain why. "Your case requires a different approach — possibly work with a psychologist, not a hypnotherapist," or "I don't specialize in this type of trauma; you need a colleague with different experience."
  3. Refer. If possible — give a contact for a verified specialist who can help. Don't leave the client alone with their problem.
  4. Refund the money. This is a fundamental point. If you couldn't help — you have no right to keep money for work that wasn't done. Refunding is not a gesture of goodwill, but professional ethics.

Example Conversation

Therapist: "Svetlana, I want to be honest with you. We've had three sessions, and I see that my techniques aren't producing the desired effect. I believe your case requires work with a psychologist specializing in trauma — that's not my area. I'll refund you for these sessions and give you contacts of three trusted colleagues who can help you."

Client: "But I want to work with you! Can't we try more?"

Therapist: "I appreciate your trust, but continuing sessions when I see they're ineffective would be unethical on my part. I'd rather spend that time finding a specialist who can truly help you."

The client may be upset, may insist, may even get angry. That's normal. Your job is to maintain a professional position. Don't give in to persuasion, don't promise what you can't deliver. An honest refusal is respect for the client and for yourself.

And remember: one case where you honestly refused money and referred the client to the right specialist builds your reputation more than ten successful sessions. Clients talk about honest therapists. And reputation is everything.

✍️ Practice: Practicing the Refusal

Exercise. Pair work or independent.

  1. Come up with a scenario where you realize you can't help a client (e.g.: client is your mother, client is 30 years older than you, client has trauma related to your gender).
  2. Write the text of "the talk" — exactly what you'll say to the client. Use the structure: honest acknowledgment → explanation → referral → refund.
  3. Say the text aloud (if working in a pair — your partner plays the client's role).
  4. Check: does the text sound confident and respectful? Are there any apologies or excuses? You don't need those. Just facts.

Success criterion: your text takes no more than 2–3 sentences, contains no excuses, clearly states the reason for refusal, and offers an alternative.

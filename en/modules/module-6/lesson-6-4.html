6.4 — Speed vs Depth

Module 6 • 15 min

🎬 Video will be inserted after uploading to YouTube

⏱️ The Dilemma of Speed and Depth

One of the most common questions from beginning hypnotherapists: "How fast should I be able to induce trance?" And the second: "Won't fast induction be shallow?" Both questions have a clear answer: professionalism is not speed in itself, but the ability to choose the right speed for the task.

When a hypnotist is just learning, they spend 15–20 minutes on induction. With experience, this time decreases. A professional can induce deep trance in 2–3 minutes, and in some cases — in 10 seconds. But it's not about being "fastest," it's about choosing the right speed for the specific client in the specific situation.

⚡ Professionalism = Speed

The principle is stated quite bluntly: "If you know what you're doing — you can do it quickly." Slow inductions are not a sign of depth, but a sign of uncertainty or poor technique.

Speed in hypnosis is not a race, but a marker of mastery. An experienced hypnotherapist can:

  • Establish rapport in 1–2 minutes.
  • Conduct a quick suggestibility test.
  • Choose the appropriate technique on the fly.
  • Induce trance in 30–60 seconds (rapid induction) or 5–10 seconds (instant).
  • Deepen and check the state in 1–2 minutes.
  • Deliver the suggestion and bring the client out — another 2–3 minutes.

Speed also gives confidence to the client: when the therapist works quickly and clearly, the client has no doubt that "this works." Slow, uncertain movements, on the other hand, increase skepticism.

But — and this is a crucial "but" — speed must not become rushing. Rushing is when you're in a hurry to finish the session, miss signs of trance, ignore the client's state. Speed is when you perform each action as efficiently as possible, not wasting extra time on what's unnecessary.

🧘 Speed Is Not the Goal — the Client Needs an Experience

An important caveat: speed is a tool, not a goal. The main goal of hypnotherapy is not to induce trance faster than anyone else, but to create for the client an experience of an altered state that they recognize and remember.

What does this mean in practice? The client needs to:

  • Feel that "something happened." If the induction was so fast that the client "didn't notice anything," they may conclude hypnosis doesn't work.
  • Experience the state of trance consciously. Especially in the first session — so the client develops an understanding of what trance is and loses the fear of "losing control."
  • Get confirmation. Eyelid catalepsy, hand levitation, changes in body sensation — the client must see and feel that trance has occurred.

Therefore, speed should be dosed: in the first sessions — slower with demonstration of effects, in subsequent sessions — faster and shorter. The client gets used to the state and no longer needs as much "proof."

📋 Strategy: From Full Protocol to Instant Induction

There is a clear strategy for progression of speed across sessions. It allows both the client to get used to trance and the therapist to save time on repeat sessions.

🟢 First Session — Full Elman

In the first session, use the full Elman protocol — with all stages: eye fixation, rapport building, eye closure, relaxation of eyelids and face, hand levitation, deepening by count, checking catalepsy and analgesia.

Why this is necessary:

  • The client experiences the whole process and understands what hypnosis looks like.
  • You check their hypnotizability — how deep they go, what trance signs they show.
  • The client sees concrete evidence: hand catalepsy, insensitivity to a pinch.
  • Trust is built in the method and the therapist.

The first session takes 45–60 minutes, of which 15–20 are for induction and deepening, the rest for working with the request and discussion.

🟡 Second Session — Abbreviated Elman

In the second session, you shorten the induction. Usually this takes 5–10 minutes instead of 20. What's removed:

  • Eye fixation — can be skipped if an anchor remains from the first session.
  • Detailed explanations — the client already knows what will happen.
  • Slow relaxation — can go through body zones faster.

What remains: hand levitation (as a readiness check), deepening by count, catalepsy. You save time but keep the key anchors the client already knows.

🔵 Third+ Session — Rapid or Instant Induction

In the third and subsequent sessions, use rapid induction (30–60 seconds) or instant (5–10 seconds).

What it looks like in practice:

  • You say: "Remember how we entered trance last session. Just close your eyes... and take a deep breath... and you're already there, deeper and deeper."
  • Use a signal anchor: a touch on the shoulder, a word, a finger snap.
  • The client enters trance in 10–30 seconds because their subconscious already knows the way.

Important condition: instant induction only works if rapport is already established and the client trusts the therapist. Trying instant induction in the first session is a beginner's mistake.

🎯 Adapting to the Client

The strategy described above is a baseline scenario that needs to be adapted to the specific client. There's no universal protocol, only individual approach.

🧠 For Analysts — Faster

Analytical clients — those who control, analyze, and check everything — often speed up the induction themselves. If you start slowly relaxing each muscle, they'll get bored and start resisting. Work faster with them, using:

  • Paradoxical commands: "You want to control everything so much that you won't let yourself relax."
  • Overload: "Notice three things at the same time..." — to load the analytical mind and bypass it.
  • Instant inductions: finger snap, handshake with fixation.

Analysts often doubt — but once they enter trance, they go very deep. Their skepticism is a defense, not an inability for trance.

🐢 For Slow Clients — Slower

There are clients who are "slow" by nature. They speak slowly, move slowly, take a long time to process information. Work with them slower than usual. If you try to speed up — they'll fall out of the process.

Techniques for slow clients:

  • Very smooth, languid voice.
  • Long pauses between phrases.
  • Gradual relaxation of each body part.
  • More time to "live through" each stage.

"Don't try to fit the client into your pace. Adapt to their pace. If you work fast and the client is slow — you lose contact."

👁️ When It's Important to Show the Effect

In some situations, demonstrating hypnotic phenomena is not an option, but a necessity. Especially if the client is skeptical, fearful, or doubts whether "this even works."

What phenomena can and should be shown:

  • Eyelid catalepsy. "Try to open your eyes" — the eyelids don't obey. Simple, visual, and impressive. Works on 95% of clients in medium and deep trance.
  • Hand catalepsy (arm as stone). The hand becomes rigid, impossible to bend. The client sees: "my body reacts, even though I'm doing nothing."
  • Hand levitation. The hand rises on its own, without effort. Shows that the subconscious is "leading" the body.
  • Analgesia. An area of skin loses sensitivity. Can be shown with a pinch or a blunt needle (with the client's consent).
  • Number/name amnesia. Temporary forgetting of simple information — a demonstrative test of trance depth.

When demonstration is mandatory:

  • In the first session — to build trust.
  • With skeptics — to give "proof" to their rational mind.
  • With clients afraid of hypnosis — to show that trance is safe.
  • With "unhypnotizable" clients — to convince them otherwise.

But — an important caveat: demonstrating phenomena is not a show. Don't make the client into a circus pony. Every demonstration should serve a therapeutic purpose: strengthen trust, deepen trance, prepare for suggestion. If you show catalepsy "just because you can" — you're sliding into stage hypnosis, not doing therapy.

✍️ Practice: Planning Induction Speed

Exercise. Take one request (e.g., "quit smoking") and create a plan for three sessions where induction speed progresses from full protocol to instant.

  1. Session 1. Describe the full induction protocol, including checking catalepsy and analgesia. What phenomena will you show? How will you explain to the client that trance has occurred?
  2. Session 2. Describe the abbreviated protocol. What will you remove? What will you keep? What anchor will you set for acceleration?
  3. Session 3. Describe instant induction. What signal will trigger trance? How will you check depth?

Success criterion: For each session, specific induction time, specific steps, and specific trance signs you check are indicated. The plan should not take more than one page.

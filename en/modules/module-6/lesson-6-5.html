6.5 — Ethics and Boundaries

Module 6 • 15 min

⚖️ What Is Ethics in Hypnotherapy

Ethics in hypnosis is not an abstract set of "rules of good conduct." It is a set of working principles that determine who, how, and why you can help. Violating ethical boundaries turns hypnotherapy into manipulation, and the therapist into a fraud. This issue receives significant attention because hypnosis masters bear special responsibility: you gain access to a person's subconscious, and this access must only be used for good.

Ethics is not what you "should" do to be good. It is what protects both you and the client. Without ethical boundaries, you risk causing harm, losing your reputation, and — in the worst case — ending up in court. With them, you build a sustainable practice to which clients return and which they recommend.

🩺 Do No Harm — The Main Principle

The first and foremost ethical principle of hypnotherapy — "do no harm" (Primum non nocere). Hypnosis is a powerful tool, and like any powerful tool, it can both help and harm. Not because hypnosis is "dangerous," but because incorrect application of any technique can have negative consequences.

Important facts every hypnotherapist should know:

  • Hypnosis doesn't give superpowers. You cannot make a person do something that contradicts their core values. A person's moral compass remains with them even in deep trance.
  • You cannot force someone to commit a crime. All experiments (both classical and modern) show: a suggestion that contradicts a person's moral principles either doesn't work or causes strong emotional discomfort and emergence from trance.
  • You cannot "break" a psyche with hypnosis. But you can worsen existing problems if you work unprofessionally — for example, trying to "pull out" trauma without preparation.
  • Hypnosis doesn't erase memory. Temporary amnesia is possible (post-hypnotic), but it's a reversible process. You cannot "erase" memories permanently.

The "do no harm" principle means that your first duty is to assess whether your work will cause harm. If there's even the slightest doubt — don't work until you understand the situation.

📝 Informed Consent

The second fundamental principle — informed consent. The client must understand:

  • What hypnosis really is (not like in the movies).
  • How the session will proceed.
  • What sensations to expect.
  • What their rights are — to stop the session at any time, refuse a suggestion, ask a question.
  • That hypnosis does not give complete control over them.

Informed consent is not just signing a piece of paper. It's a conversation in which you remove fears and myths, explain a realistic picture, and obtain the conscious consent of the client's subconscious to work.

How to explain hypnosis to a client:

  • Hypnosis is bypassing the critical factor, not losing control. "Your conscious mind will rest, while your subconscious will work. You will hear my voice and can emerge from this state at any time simply by opening your eyes."
  • You will not lose consciousness or fall asleep. "You'll be in a state of deep relaxation and focused attention — like when you watch an interesting movie and lose track of time."
  • You won't do anything against your will. "Even in deep trance, your subconscious won't accept a suggestion that contradicts your values."

If after the explanation the client is still afraid — don't push. Offer to try a light relaxation without hypnosis so they can experience for themselves that it's safe. Only after the client has consciously agreed can you begin work.

🚧 Boundaries: What You Cannot Work With Without Medical Education

There is a clear boundary: a hypnotherapist is not a doctor unless they have medical education. This means there is a category of problems a hypnotherapist has no right to take on.

Strictly forbidden to take on:

  • Organic diseases. Tumors, infections, autoimmune diseases, organ damage. Hypnosis does not treat physical illnesses — it can help with psychosomatic components, but only under medical supervision.
  • Psychiatric disorders (acute). Schizophrenia, bipolar disorder in acute phase, psychoses. Hypnosis can trigger worsening of the condition.
  • Epilepsy (without a neurologist's approval). Some hypnosis techniques (flickering light, sudden suggestions) can trigger a seizure.
  • Diagnosing diseases. A hypnotherapist does not give medical diagnoses — even if the client asks "to see what's hurting me."

What you can do:

  • Work with psychosomatics — if the client has already been examined by a doctor and no organic cause was found.
  • Work with chronic pain — under the supervision of the attending physician.
  • Work with concomitant issues — stress, anxiety, insomnia related to an illness — with the doctor's permission.

If a client comes with back pain and hasn't seen a doctor — your first recommendation: go to a doctor. Don't treat pain with hypnosis until organic causes are ruled out. This isn't stinginess, it's responsibility.

🚫 Do Not Use Manipulation

Returning to the theme of "gypsy hypnosis" to emphasize: any manipulation is a violation of ethics. The difference between hypnotherapy and manipulation lies in consent and purpose.

Signs of manipulation in hypnosis:

  • You use hypnosis for personal gain at the client's expense. For example: pushing additional services, selling "miracle courses" under hypnosis.
  • You hide the true goal of the suggestion. The client thinks you're working with anxiety, but you're actually suggesting loyalty to or dependence on you.
  • You give suggestions without consent. Even if the suggestion is "beneficial" — you have no right to make it without the client's explicit consent.
  • You create dependency. The client can't enter trance "without you," or you suggest that only you can help them.

"The gypsy approach" is not hypnosis, and any hypnotherapist who uses manipulative techniques discredits the profession. An honest hypnotherapist does not suggest what wasn't agreed upon with the client. Every suggestion should be agreed upon, explained, and accepted by the client at a conscious level — even if technically you're working with the subconscious.

🔄 When to Refer to Another Specialist

A hypnotherapist's professional maturity is shown not by how many clients they "took on," but by how many they correctly referred. Cases where your job is not to work but to redirect:

Situation Where to Refer
Psychotrauma (violence, loss, PTSD) To a psychologist/psychotherapist specializing in trauma
Depression (clinical), bipolar disorder, schizophrenia To a psychiatrist — medication support is primary
Pain, dizziness, other symptoms without diagnosis To a doctor — rule out organic causes
Addictions (alcohol, drugs) To an addiction specialist — hypnosis can be a supplement, not the foundation
Cases beyond your competence To a colleague with the appropriate specialization

"Don't try to be a 'rescuer.' You are a hypnotherapist — you have an area of competence. If the problem goes beyond it — your job is to refer, not to heroically fail."

Additionally, it's important to have a list of trusted specialists to whom you refer clients: psychologists, psychiatrists, doctors of various specialties. This is not a one-time action, but part of your professional infrastructure.

🏛️ Reputation — The Hypnotherapist's Capital

Concluding the ethics lesson, an important practical observation: in hypnotherapy, reputation is everything. One bad case can erase years of work.

Why reputation is so important:

  • Word of mouth. Hypnosis is a field where clients mostly come through referrals. One dissatisfied client will tell ten people. A satisfied one — maybe won't tell anyone.
  • The "miracle" effect. People often expect a "miracle" from hypnotherapy. If the miracle doesn't happen — disappointment will be strong, and the client will tell everyone that "hypnosis doesn't work."
  • Public skepticism. Hypnosis is still surrounded by myths and prejudices. One scandalous case — and you become "another quack" in the eyes of potential clients.

How to maintain your reputation:

  1. Don't promise what you can't guarantee. Don't say "I'll cure you in one session." Say "I'll help you with this problem — let's try and see the result."
  2. Refund money if you couldn't help. The client will leave with a feeling of honesty, not deception.
  3. Refer when you can't help. The client will appreciate that you care about their well-being, not your pocket.
  4. Document your work. Record the request, consent, session progress, results. This protects you in case of complaints.
  5. Improve your qualifications. Learn, practice, consult with colleagues. A valuable specialist is a forever-learning specialist.

"Reputation takes years to build and one day to destroy. One thoughtless step — and you'll lose the trust you've gathered for years. Remember: you work with people, not clients. Every person who comes to you for help deserves the respect and care you would want for yourself."

✍️ Practice: Developing an Ethics Code

Exercise. Create your own hypnotherapist's ethics code — 5–7 points you will follow in your work.

Consider the following questions:

  1. What problems do I work with, and which do I not?
  2. How do I obtain the client's consent?
  3. What do I do when I realize I can't help?
  4. How do I handle confidential information?
  5. What do I do if a client suggests or asks me to violate ethical boundaries?

Example code point: "I do not work with clients who haven't undergone medical examination when physical symptoms are present. My first recommendation is a doctor's consultation."

Success criterion: The code takes no more than one page, is formulated specifically (not "I will be good," but "I do not work with..."), and you are ready to sign it and show it to clients.
